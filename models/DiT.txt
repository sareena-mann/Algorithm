DIFFUSION TRANSFORMER (DiT) — DEEP ARCHITECTURE WALKTHROUGH
=============================================================
File: arch/dit.py


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 1: WHAT THE MODEL IS TRYING TO DO
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The DiT is a noise predictor. It is never asked to "create music."
It is only ever asked one question:

  "Given this noisy bridge, these two context windows, and this noise level —
   what is the noise component buried inside the bridge?"

That's it. All the creativity and musical coherence emerges from training that
single skill 20,000+ times across diverse transitions.

During inference, you start with pure random noise and chip away at it step by step.
After 1000 steps of "remove a little noise, remove a little more," what remains is
a coherent bridge that flows from Track A into Track B.

This technique is called Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Modeling (DDPM).
The exact same family of models powers Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 3, and Suno.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 2: THE NOISE SCHEDULE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Constants:
  T_STEPS = 1000   — the total number of noise levels
  ALPHA_BAR        — a tensor of 1000 values, one per timestep
  ALPHA            — the per-step noise multiplier derived from ALPHA_BAR

The schedule is computed by _cosine_alphas():

  f(t) = cos( ((t/T) + 0.008) / 1.008 × π/2 )²

  alpha_bar[t] = f(t) / f(0)

alpha_bar[t] is the fraction of the signal that remains at timestep t.
At t=0:   alpha_bar ≈ 1.0  → almost no noise, signal mostly intact
At t=999: alpha_bar ≈ 0.0  → pure noise, signal completely destroyed

Why cosine instead of linear?
  A linear schedule adds noise at a constant rate. A cosine schedule adds noise
  slowly at first (preserving fine musical detail longer), then accelerates,
  then slows again at the very end. This gives the model more gradient signal on
  the subtle structure of real music — the attack of a kick drum, the tail of a
  reverb — rather than spending most of its time on coarse structure.
  The 0.008 offset prevents the schedule from hitting exactly 0 or 1, which
  causes numerical instability in log space.

These tensors are computed once at module load time and stored as globals
(ALPHA_BAR, ALPHA) so they don't get recomputed every forward pass.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 3: SINUSOIDAL EMBEDDING (SinusoidalEmb)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Input:  t — a batch of integers, shape (B,), values in [0, 999]
  Output: shape (B, model_dim)

The model needs to know what noise level it's operating at. But a raw integer
like "437" is meaningless to a neural network — it has no geometric relationship
to "438" unless you encode it carefully.

Sinusoidal embedding encodes each integer as a point in a high-dimensional
space where nearby integers are nearby vectors:

  Step 1: Create dim/2 frequencies, logarithmically spaced from 1 to 10000.
          freqs[i] = exp( -log(10000) × i / (dim/2 - 1) )
          Low-index freqs are high-frequency (change fast across timesteps).
          High-index freqs are low-frequency (change slowly across timesteps).

  Step 2: For each element in the batch, multiply t by each frequency:
          emb[b, i] = t[b] × freqs[i]

  Step 3: Concatenate sine and cosine of those products:
          output[b] = [sin(emb[b,0]), ..., sin(emb[b,dim/2-1]),
                       cos(emb[b,0]), ..., cos(emb[b,dim/2-1])]

This is the same encoding used in the original Transformer paper for positional
embeddings. The key property: the model can compute the relationship between
any two timesteps from their embeddings alone, without ever having to memorize
specific integer values.

After SinusoidalEmb, two linear layers with SiLU activation expand and refine
the embedding into a full model_dim=512 vector. This final vector is what gets
added to the conditioning.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 4: ADAPTIVE LAYER NORM (AdaLN)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Input:  x    — the token sequence, shape (B, T, model_dim)
          cond — the combined conditioning vector, shape (B, model_dim)
  Output: shape (B, T, model_dim)

This is the most important innovation in the DiT architecture. In a standard
transformer, Layer Norm just normalizes each token's values to zero mean and
unit variance — it has no awareness of external information. AdaLN goes further:
it uses the conditioning vector to dynamically shift and scale every token after
normalization.

Step by step:

  Step 1: Normalize x with standard LayerNorm, but with elementwise_affine=False.
          This means the norm has no learnable gamma/beta — those are replaced by
          the adaptive parameters in the next step.
          After this: each token has mean=0, std=1 across the model_dim dimension.

  Step 2: Project the conditioning vector through a single Linear layer:
            proj(cond) → shape (B, 2 × model_dim)
          Split this into two halves:
            scale: (B, model_dim)
            shift: (B, model_dim)

  Step 3: Apply to the normalized tokens:
            output = normalized_x × (1 + scale) + shift
          The unsqueeze makes scale and shift broadcast across the T dimension,
          so every token in the sequence gets the same adaptive transformation.

Why this matters for the DJ transition task:
  At timestep t=999 (almost pure noise), the conditioning should tell every layer
  "be aggressive, make big corrections." At t=10 (nearly clean audio), the
  conditioning should say "be careful, make tiny refinements." AdaLN is how
  that timestep-dependent behavior gets communicated — not just at the input,
  but inside every single transformer block. The model at every depth simultaneously
  knows how noisy the signal is and what kind of transition it's building.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 5: DiTBLOCK
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

One DiTBlock is a single transformer layer. The model stacks 12 of them.
Each block has two sub-layers: self-attention and a feedforward network.
Both use AdaLN for normalization (pre-norm style, meaning normalize before
computing attention/feedforward, not after).

  Input:  x    — token sequence, shape (B, T_total, model_dim)
          cond — conditioning vector, shape (B, model_dim)
  Output: shape (B, T_total, model_dim)  [same shape — residual architecture]

SUB-LAYER 1: Self-Attention

  h = attn_norm(x, cond)       — AdaLN: normalize and condition
  h, _ = attn(h, h, h)         — MultiheadAttention with 8 heads
  x = x + h                    — residual connection

  The three h arguments to attn() are Query, Key, and Value — all the same
  tensor (self-attention). Every token in the sequence computes attention scores
  against every other token. This is what allows a bridge token at position 200
  to "look at" context_A tokens at positions 0-1290 and context_B tokens at
  positions 1550-2160. The full cross-window attention is what forces the bridge
  to be musically coherent with both tracks simultaneously.

  With 8 heads, the attention is split into 8 parallel subspaces of 64 dimensions
  each (512 / 8 = 64). Each head can specialize: one head might learn to track
  harmonic relationships (key), another might track rhythmic structure (BPM),
  another might track energy envelope.

  Residual connection: the output is added back to the original x. This means
  if the attention sub-layer learns nothing useful, it can output zero and the
  block passes x through unchanged. This prevents early training instability.

SUB-LAYER 2: Feedforward Network

  x = x + ff(ff_norm(x, cond))

  The feedforward is a two-layer MLP applied independently to each token:
    Linear(512 → 2048) → GELU → Dropout(0.1) → Linear(2048 → 512)

  The 4× expansion (512 → 2048) is standard transformer design. The wider
  intermediate layer gives the network capacity to compute complex non-linear
  transformations per token. GELU is preferred over ReLU in transformers because
  its smooth gradient near zero helps optimization.

  Dropout(0.1) randomly zeros 10% of neurons during training, which prevents
  the network from memorizing specific training examples and forces it to learn
  more general musical patterns.

  Another residual connection wraps the feedforward. Both residuals together mean
  gradient flows cleanly from the output all the way back to the input during
  backpropagation, making training of 12+ layer networks feasible.

PART 6: THE FULL MODEL — TransitionDiT.forward()
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This is the entire forward pass, step by step.

INPUT TENSORS:
  context_a    : (B, T_a,      64)   latents from end of Track A
  noisy_bridge : (B, T_bridge, 64)   clean bridge + added noise
  context_b    : (B, T_b,      64)   latents from start of Track B
  timestep     : (B,)                integer noise level per example
  conditioning : (B, 28)             BPM/key/genre vector

  T_a      ≈ 1290 tokens (15 seconds × 86 tokens/sec)
  T_bridge ≈  344–860 tokens (4–10 seconds × 86 tokens/sec)
  T_b      ≈  860 tokens (10 seconds × 86 tokens/sec)

STEP 1: Project latents from codec dimension to model dimension

  a  = in_proj(context_a)    # Linear(64 → 512):  (B, T_a,      512)
  br = in_proj(noisy_bridge) # Linear(64 → 512):  (B, T_bridge, 512)
  b  = in_proj(context_b)    # Linear(64 → 512):  (B, T_b,      512)

  The codec latents are 64-dimensional. The transformer needs 512-dimensional
  tokens to have enough capacity for 8-head attention. This linear projection
  is a learned dimension expansion — the model learns which directions in the
  512-dim space are most useful for each latent feature.

STEP 2: Add segment embeddings

  a  += seg_embed(0)   — tells every context_A token "you are from Track A"
  br += seg_embed(1)   — tells every bridge token "you are the transition"
  b  += seg_embed(2)   — tells every context_B token "you are from Track B"

  seg_embed is an nn.Embedding(3, 512) — a lookup table with three rows.
  These are learned during training. Without segment embeddings, the model
  cannot distinguish whether position 500 is a late context_A token or an
  early bridge token. With them, every token carries a permanent "identity tag"
  that persists through all 12 transformer layers.

  This is especially critical during inference: the model must know which tokens
  it is being asked to denoise (the bridge) vs. which tokens are ground truth
  anchors (the contexts). The attention mechanism can then learn to use context
  tokens as reference and bridge tokens as output targets.

STEP 3: Concatenate into one sequence

  seq = cat([a, br, b], dim=1)  # (B, T_a + T_bridge + T_b, 512)

  This is the full input to the transformer. All tokens — from Track A, the
  bridge, and Track B — are packed into a single flat sequence. The transformer's
  self-attention has O(T²) complexity, so every token attends to every other.
  A bridge token at position T_a + 50 can attend to a context_A token at
  position 10 AND a context_B token at position T_a + T_bridge + 200.
  This global attention is what makes the bridge musically coherent at both ends.

STEP 4: Build the conditioning vector

  cond = t_embed(timestep) + c_embed(conditioning)  # (B, 512)

  t_embed processes the diffusion timestep through SinusoidalEmb + 2 linear layers.
  c_embed processes the 28-dim BPM/key/genre vector through 2 linear layers.
  They are simply added together into one 512-dim vector.

  This combined vector is what gets passed to every AdaLN in every DiTBlock.
  The model simultaneously knows: "I am denoising at level 437, building a
  transition from 124 BPM house (8A) to 128 BPM techno (9A)."

STEP 5: Pass through 12 DiTBlocks

  for block in blocks:
      seq = block(seq, cond)

  Each block: AdaLN → 8-head self-attention → residual → AdaLN → FFN → residual.
  The conditioning is injected fresh into every AdaLN in every block.
  After 12 layers, every token has been refined 12 times with full knowledge
  of the global context (all other tokens) and the conditioning (noise level,
  BPM, key, genre).

  The first few layers tend to learn local structure (a token attends strongly
  to its neighbors). The middle layers learn medium-range relationships (beat
  alignment across a few seconds). The later layers learn global structure
  (the energy arc of the entire transition).

STEP 6: Extract the bridge slice and project back to latent dim

  out = out_norm(seq[:, T_a : T_a + T_bridge, :])   # (B, T_bridge, 512)
  return out_proj(out)                                # (B, T_bridge, 64)

  The transformer outputs a refined representation for all tokens. We only
  care about the bridge slice — context_A and context_B are anchors, not outputs.
  A final LayerNorm stabilizes the values, then a linear layer projects back
  down from 512 to 64 (the codec latent dimension).

  The result is the predicted noise — what the model thinks was added to the
  clean bridge to produce the noisy bridge it was given.

ZERO INITIALIZATION OF out_proj:
  Both the weight matrix and bias of the final linear layer are initialized to
  zero. This means at the very start of training, before any learning has
  happened, the model predicts zero noise for every input. This is beneficial
  because the loss at initialization is then just the variance of the target
  (the actual noise), rather than an arbitrarily large random prediction.
  The model starts from a known, stable baseline and improves from there.

PART 7: add_noise() — FORWARD DIFFUSION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Used during training only.

  Input:  x0 — clean bridge latents, shape (B, T_bridge, 64)
          t  — random timesteps, shape (B,), values in [0, 999]
  Output: (x_t, epsilon) — the noisy version and the noise that was added

The forward diffusion equation is:

  x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) × x0  +  sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) × epsilon

  where epsilon ~ N(0, I)  (standard Gaussian noise)

This is a convex combination of the clean signal and pure noise.
At t=0:   sqrt(alpha_bar) ≈ 1.0, so x_t ≈ x0        (almost clean)
At t=999: sqrt(alpha_bar) ≈ 0.0, so x_t ≈ epsilon    (almost pure noise)

The "while alpha_bar.dim() < x0.dim()" loop adds dimensions to alpha_bar
so it broadcasts correctly against the (B, T, D) shape of x0. This is a
standard PyTorch pattern for applying per-batch scalars to batched tensors.

After add_noise(), training does:
  loss = MSE(model(x_t, context_a, context_b, t, cond), epsilon)

The model is trained to recover epsilon from x_t. Because we kept epsilon
around, we can compute this loss exactly — no approximations needed.


━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
PART 8: ddpm_sample() — INFERENCE / GENERATION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Used during inference only. No gradients computed (@torch.no_grad()).

  Input:  model        — the trained TransitionDiT
          context_a    — latents of Track A's ending (1, T_a, 64)
          context_b    — latents of Track B's beginning (1, T_b, 64)
          conditioning — BPM/key/genre vector (1, 28)
          bridge_len   — number of bridge tokens to generate
  Output: bridge latents (1, bridge_len, 64)

Step 1: Initialize with pure noise.
  x = torch.randn(1, bridge_len, 64)
  This is the starting point: a completely random, meaningless signal.

Step 2: Iterate from t=999 down to t=0.
  For each timestep t:

  a) Ask the model to predict the noise:
       eps_pred = model(context_a, x, context_b, t, conditioning)

  b) Use eps_pred to estimate what the clean bridge would look like right now:
       x0_pred = (x - sqrt(1-alpha_bar[t]) × eps_pred) / sqrt(alpha_bar[t])
     This is an algebraic rearrangement of the forward diffusion equation.
     x0_pred is a rough estimate — at high noise levels it's very inaccurate,
     but it gets better with every denoising step.
     Clamping to [-4, 4] prevents numerical blow-up in early steps when the
     model's estimates are unreliable.

  c) Compute the posterior mean for x at the previous timestep (t-1):
       mean = (sqrt(alpha[t]) × alpha_bar[t-1]) / (1 - alpha_bar[t]) × x
            + (sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t-1]) × (1 - alpha[t])) / (1 - alpha_bar[t]) × x0_pred
     This is the DDPM reverse step formula. It combines the current noisy x
     with the rough clean estimate x0_pred, weighted by how much signal vs.
     noise is expected at each timestep.

  d) Add a small amount of fresh Gaussian noise (for all steps except the last):
       variance = (1 - alpha_bar[t-1]) / (1 - alpha_bar[t]) × (1 - alpha[t])
       x = mean + sqrt(variance) × randn_like(x)
     Adding noise at each step (except the final) is what makes DDPM stochastic —
     two inference runs from different random seeds produce different but equally
     valid bridges. This mirrors the diversity of human DJ transitions.
     At t=0, no noise is added — the final output is the deterministic mean.

After 1000 iterations, x has been transformed from pure noise into coherent
bridge latents that are conditioned on both context tracks and the BPM/key/genre
vector. These get passed to codec.decode() to produce the final audio.

PART 9: PARAMETER COUNT AND CAPACITY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

With the default configuration (model_dim=512, n_heads=8, n_layers=12):

  in_proj:         64 × 512                        =    32,768
  seg_embed:       3 × 512                         =     1,536
  t_embed:         512² + 512×2048 + 2048×512      = 2,359,296  (approx)
  c_embed:         28×512 + 512×512                =   276,992
  12 × DiTBlock:
    AdaLN proj:    2 × (512 × 1024)                = 1,048,576  each block
    Attention QKV: 3 × 512 × 512                   =   786,432  each block
    Attention out: 512 × 512                       =   262,144  each block
    FFN:           512×2048 + 2048×512             = 2,097,152  each block
    Total per block:                               ≈ 4,194,304
  12 blocks:                                       ≈50,331,648
  out_norm + out_proj:                             ≈   263,168

  TOTAL: approximately 53 million parameters

For comparison: GPT-2 small is 117M. This model is intentionally compact —
it only generates 4–10 seconds of audio, not paragraphs of text. 53M parameters
is enough capacity to learn DJ transition structure while still being trainable
on a MacBook M-series chip in reasonable time.

PART 10: DATA FLOW DIAGRAM
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

TRAINING:
                    clean bridge (B, T_br, 64)
                           │
              random t ──► add_noise()
                           │
                    noisy bridge (B, T_br, 64)
                           │
  context_a (B,T_a,64) ──►│◄── context_b (B,T_b,64)
  timestep (B,)       ──►│
  conditioning (B,28) ──►│
                           │
                    TransitionDiT.forward()
                           │
                    eps_predicted (B, T_br, 64)
                           │
                    MSE vs. actual epsilon
                           │
                        loss.backward()


INFERENCE:
  pure noise (1, bridge_len, 64)
           │
           │  ×1000 iterations (t=999 → 0):
           │
           │   context_a ──►│
           │   context_b ──►│◄── TransitionDiT
           │   t        ──►│
           │   cond     ──►│
           │        eps_pred
           │           │
           │     DDPM reverse step
           │           │
           └───────────┘ (x gets cleaner each step)
           │
    clean bridge latents (1, bridge_len, 64)
           │
      codec.decode()
           │
    bridge audio (1, 1, T_samples)
